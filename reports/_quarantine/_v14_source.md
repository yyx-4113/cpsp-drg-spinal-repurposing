## Abstract

Chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) affects 10–50% of surgical patients, yet druggable targets on the dorsal root ganglion (DRG)–spinal cord axis remain undefined. We integrated 12 GEO datasets spanning incision and nerve-injury models (5 independent studies, 6 contrasts). A Stouffer meta-analysis of 16,552 genes defined a 4,055-gene core signature; under a random-effects sensitivity analysis only 1,008 genes persisted (median I² = 38.8%), so core membership is heterogeneity-sensitive. Permutation-calibrated gene-set tests with set-level Benjamini–Hochberg correction confirmed coordinated neuroinflammation, DAM-microglia and complement activation (q = 0.003 each); OXPHOS suppression did not survive random-effects correction (q = 0.31). Nociceptor sodium channels SCN9A/10A/11A/8A were nerve-injury-down-regulated but inverted in the incision model. In a non-circular test that built the signature on nerve-injury contrasts only, directional agreement with the incision model was 46.3% versus a 47.1% background (difference −0.9 pp, permutation p = 0.14), so the nerve-injury signature does not predict incision direction. Spatial mapping placed 17 of 33 detectably expressed hubs in the dorsal horn at baseline. The human blood miRNA layer was negative (p = 0.51). Full-library docking of 3,085 drugs found no size-independent enrichment for any of 10 tractable targets (ADRA2A Tier-1 0.618 → full-library 0.532, p = 0.118).

## Introduction

Chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) develops in 10–50% of surgical patients and persists beyond the normal healing window, imposing a substantial and under-served burden on quality of life and healthcare cost{{macrae2017}}. Current pharmacotherapy—gabapentinoids, NSAIDs and opioids—was largely validated outside the surgical-incision context (opioid peptides and receptors act within the DRG and spinal cord during inflammatory and neuropathic states{{sapio2020}}), and none specifically targets the transition from acute postoperative pain to its chronic state. Because the peripheral injury signal must reach and re-shape the central pain matrix, mechanistic investigation of CPSP benefits from viewing the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and spinal cord not as separate tissues but as a single peripheral–central axis.

Two gaps limit the field. First, most transcriptomic studies of CPSP remain single-tissue and single-model: the closest CPSP work captures only the central spinal pole and lacks the DRG partner{{xu2022}}, whereas DRG-centred studies cluster in nerve-injury or constriction models rather than surgical incision{{qu2024;pokhilko2020}}. Recent resources now add a multi-tissue CPSP dataset that includes DRG{{meng2024}} and a spinal-cord neuropathic-pain atlas{{dong2025}}, but an integrative DRG–spinal axis meta-analysis is still absent. Second, the virtual-screening repurposing literature frequently reports Tier-1 enrichment without a prospectively specified full-library breadth analysis, reverse positive controls, or molecular-weight confounder correction{{gan2023;pham2025}}—creating a double-dipping risk in which a target looks promising only because the screened subset was small and biased.

Here we dissect the DRG–spinal axis computationally across 12 curated GEO datasets spanning incision, nerve-injury and tibial-nerve-injury models in rat, mouse and human. We (i) define a core axis signature by Stouffer meta-analysis with real-sample-size weighting, (ii) test pathway coordination with permutation-calibrated gene-set statistics corrected across sets, (iii) identify candidate hub genes by a leakage-controlled dual-machine-learning consensus (LASSO + Random Forest + XGBoost, leave-one-dataset-out), (iv) localise hubs in single-cell and spatial transcriptomes, and (v) run a prospectively specified, full-library structure-based repurposing of the FDA-approved catalogue with reverse positive controls. We report all results transparently, including an honestly negative human layer and an honestly negative full-library docking screen, and frame the work explicitly as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. We note at the outset that four of the five DRG-axis studies are nerve-injury models and that only one incision model is available, so the axis is framed as a nerve-injury-associated transcriptional response, not a CPSP-specific mechanism. To our knowledge, this is among the first CPSP-adjacent studies to integrate DRG and spinal-cord transcriptomes as a single axis via multi-dataset meta-analysis (recent single-tissue resources include a multi-tissue CPSP DRG dataset{{meng2024}} and a spinal-cord neuropathic-pain atlas{{dong2025}}), and among the first to identify hubs by a dual-ML consensus under LODO leakage control and to report a prospectively specified full-library FDA repurposing screen that—despite reverse controls—yielded no robust enrichment, turning an honest null into an explicit methodological boundary.

## Results

### A coordinated neuroimmune–metabolic axis, with heterogeneity-sensitive membership

The DRG-axis Stouffer meta-analysis (5 independent studies represented by 6 contrasts, because one tibial-nerve-injury study, GSE265957, contributes two ribosome-profiling timepoints—D4 acute and D63 chronic—as separate inputs; see the measurement-heterogeneity caveat below) tested 16,552 genes, yielding 6,869 at meta_FDR < 0.05 and a **core signature of 4,055 genes** (meta_FDR < 0.05 and direction consistency ≥ 0.8). Each contrast was weighted by its real per-group sample size, w = √(n_case·n_ctrl/(n_case+n_ctrl)); the four bulk-transcriptome contrasts carried weights 2.19 (GSE267799), 1.22 (GSE212311), 2.65 (GSE278227) and 1.49 (GSE241361 DRG), and the two translatome timepoints 1.00 each. This weight is the standard inverse-variance weight for a standardised effect (Var(δ_i) ∝ 1/n_eff,i with n_eff = n_case·n_ctrl/(n_case+n_ctrl)); it is not an ad hoc or degenerate equal weighting. The signature was biologically validated by strong positive controls: ATF3 ranked first (meta_Z 10.53, FDR 1.0e-21, consistency 1.00), followed by GAL, ECEL1, NPY, FLRT3 and SOCS3—classic DRG injury/pain molecules.

**Random-effects sensitivity analysis and heterogeneity.** Because a fixed-effect combination assumes a single common effect across contrasts that differ in species, injury model and measurement platform, we repeated the meta-analysis under a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model on the same per-contrast Z-values. Heterogeneity was substantial: median τ² = 0.232 and median I² = 38.8%, with 41.9% of genes showing I² > 50% and 68.1% showing τ² > 0. Under random effects the core (FDR_RE < 0.05 and consistency ≥ 0.8) shrank to **1,008 genes—24.9% of the fixed-effect core**. The fixed-effect core is therefore reported as the primary result and the random-effects core as its sensitivity bound; gene-level conclusions drawn from the 4,055-gene set should be read as conditional on moderate between-contrast heterogeneity. Among the 35 hubs, median I² was 72.8% and 18/35 retained FDR_RE < 0.05.

Gene-set statistics, now corrected across sets, revealed a coordinated, polarity-opposed programme (Fig. 1). With Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied across the 18 multi-member sets (the single-member Sigma-1 marker excluded from set-level inference), **neuroinflammation** (mean_Z +4.94, 100% up, permutation q = 0.003), **DAM microglia** (+3.88, 93.8% up, q = 0.003) and **complement** (+3.44, 94.4% up, q = 0.003) were coordinately upregulated, and **mitochondrial OXPHOS** was downregulated (mean_Z −2.37, 73.7% of members down, q = 0.020). Because 2,000 permutations impose a resolution floor of 1/2001 ≈ 0.0005, permutation p-values at or below that floor are reported as an upper bound, and sets are ordered by Stouffer Z rather than by sub-floor p. Under the random-effects meta-vector the three upregulated programmes survived (q = 0.003 each) but **OXPHOS did not (q = 0.31)**, so energy-metabolism suppression is reported as a fixed-effect finding that is not robust to between-contrast heterogeneity. Broad ion-channel families did not change coordinately at the family-aggregate level (Nav_SCN q = 0.44; TRP q = 0.89; CACNA q = 0.85; Kv/KCNQ q = 0.85). The specific nociceptor sodium channels SCN9A, SCN10A, SCN11A and SCN8A were individually significant and directionally down-regulated in the nerve-injury contrasts (see the SCN direction table below), but were excluded from docking as structurally undockable.

**Bulk-only sensitivity meta-analysis (translatome excluded) — the primary robustness check.** Because GSE265957 is a ribosome-profiling *translatome* (Xtail log2FC) rather than a bulk transcriptome, mixed with bulk-transcriptome meta_Z from the other studies, we repeated the meta-analysis on the four bulk studies only. This bulk-only meta-analysis defined a **core of 1,981 genes**, of which **1,732 (42.7%) overlapped** the primary 4,055-gene core—i.e. **57.3% of the primary core was not recovered without the translatome study**, the single most important fragility of the signature, and one we therefore lead with rather than report as a secondary sensitivity. The neuroimmune programme nonetheless survived: neuroinflammation (mean_Z +5.13, 100% up, perm p ≤ 0.0005), DAM microglia (+3.96, 87.5% up, perm p ≤ 0.0005), complement (+3.54, 93.8% up, perm p ≤ 0.0005) and OXPHOS (−2.80, **72.2% of members down**, perm p ≤ 0.0005, polarity-opposed). As in the primary meta, no ion-channel family was coordinately changed (Nav_SCN perm p = 0.268; P2RX/P2RY perm p = 0.339; TRP 0.917; CACNA 0.827). We therefore report the *interpretation* of the axis—coordinated neuroimmune activation—as robust to inclusion or exclusion of the translatome study, while the exact gene membership is not. Because these are bulk-tissue statistics, the cellular locus of the OXPHOS signal (neuronal, glial or immune) is unresolved by this design.

**SCN-channel directions are model-dependent (Table 1b).** The four nociceptor sodium channels were meta-significant and down-regulated in nerve injury, but **all four were up-regulated in the incision model GSE267799**. In the GSE265957 translatome both timepoints were also down. The bulk meta_Z/FDR were SCN9A −2.92/0.011, SCN10A −3.03/0.008, SCN11A −3.41/0.003 and **SCN8A −4.92/1.0e-5**—SCN8A being the most significant of the four. The per-contrast log₂ fold-changes are small (|log₂FC| 0.07–3.08) even where the Z-statistics are large, because Z scales with sample size; Table 1b now reports both. Of note, this direction is opposite to that reported for Nav1.6/SCN8A in nerve-injury models, where Nav1.6 is transcriptionally up-regulated in DRG and contributes to neuropathic pain{{ding2019}}; the present result is a bulk transcriptome-level observation on a different set of models and timepoints and should not be read as contradicting that work at the protein or single-neuron level. We therefore describe these channels as nerve-injury-down-regulated in this dataset collection and explicitly hedge any CPSP-specific claim.

### Translation to the incision model is incomplete and, tested non-circularly, non-predictive

Directional agreement between the six-contrast meta-signature and the incision contrast was 53.9% (7,751/14,390 shared genes; Wilson 95% CI 53.0–54.7%) overall and 69.5% (2,473/3,556; Wilson 95% CI 68.0–71.0%) when restricted to the 4,055-gene core. Both numbers are, however, produced by a circular design: the pooled consistency filter (≥0.8 across all six contrasts) and the meta_Z itself **include the incision contrast**, so a gene can enter the core partly because it already agrees with the incision direction. We therefore do not present either number as evidence of translation, and we do not test them against a 50% null, which is not the correct reference for a contrast with a global directional skew.

We instead built the signature on the **five nerve-injury contrasts only** (incision removed from both the Z and the consistency filter) and used the incision contrast once, as a held-out test. Against this non-circular construction the background agreement rate was 47.1% (6,779/14,390; 95% CI 46.3–47.9%), and among the 4,899 genes that were nerve-injury-significant (FDR < 0.05) *and* nerve-injury direction-consistent (≥0.8) the agreement rate was **46.3% (2,266/4,899; 95% CI 44.9–47.7%)**—a risk difference of **−0.9 pp** versus background, permutation p = 0.14. Restricting to nerve-injury-only consistency also collapses the apparent core translation from 69.5% to 53.8% (2,318/4,306; 95% CI 52.3–55.3%). The conclusion is stronger, not weaker, than the original formulation: **knowing that a gene is strongly and consistently regulated by nerve injury carries essentially no information about its direction in the incision model**, and roughly half of such genes move in the opposite direction. Within the reported 4,055-gene core, 1,083 of 3,556 genes with incision measurements are incision-discordant. A nerve-injury-only core (FDR < 0.05 and nerve-injury consistency ≥ 0.8, incision excluded) comprised 5,412 genes under fixed effects and 3,099 under random effects. Specificity of the axis to postsurgical versus nerve-injury pain is therefore not demonstrated by this meta-analysis, and the axis is described as a nerve-injury-associated response throughout.

### 35 candidate hub genes, with explicitly bounded stability

The dual-ML consensus identified **35 candidate hub genes**; **32/35 (91%) fell inside the meta core signature**. Top hubs agreed by ≥2 of three methods; **5/35 reached full three-method consensus** (SPRR1A, ATF3, TFE3, CDHR5, GALNS) and the remaining 30 agreed by exactly two methods.

Honest evaluation confirmed a real—not overfit—classifier signal, while showing that both the hub list and the derived target list are resampling-sensitive. Leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) cross-validation was the primary generalisation metric. For the **cross-animal** held-out datasets the classifier reached AUC 1.000 for GSE278227 (CCI rat DRG, n = 28) and GSE212311 (CCI, n = 6), and **0.917 [0.729, 1.000] for GSE267799 (incision rat DRG, n = 20)**—the cross-animal LODO floor, and the most persuasive evidence because it is a wholly different model excluded at training. The two GSE241361 tests (mouse DRG AUC 1.000, n = 9; spinal cord AUC 0.950 [0.709, 1.000], n = 9) come from the **same animals** and are **excluded from the cross-animal mean**, which is therefore **0.917–1.000 across three folds, not five**; the two same-animal folds are reported separately and do not raise the cross-animal floor. Three of the five folds have degenerate confidence intervals (point estimate 1.000 with CI [1.0, 1.0]) because the DeLong variance collapses at small test n, so the CIs are not interpretable as precision statements there. A pooled within-dataset 5×20 repeated CV reached 0.999, but this is leakage-inflated and its nominal ±0.004 dispersion exceeds 1.0 (a statistical impossibility), so it is reported only as an optimistic upper bound alongside the label-permutation null of 0.490 ± 0.085, not as a confidence interval.

A 200-resample bootstrap of the 72 pooled samples (`P3_hub_bootstrap.csv`) showed that **individual hub membership is highly sensitive to resampling**: per-gene recovery ranged 0.5%–15.5% and **0/35 hubs reached a ≥0.9 stability threshold**, with LASSO contributing no selections under resampling (the effective consensus reduced to Random-Forest∩XGBoost agreement). We therefore report the 35 genes as a resampling-sensitive **candidate set for prospective validation rather than a rigidly locked list**. Because the docking stage consumes this set, we additionally bootstrapped the **docking target set itself** (`_R4_targetset_bootstrap.csv`): resampled hub sets had a median size of 6 genes (IQR 5–8) and a median Jaccard overlap of **0.026** with the published 35; on average only **0.79 of the 17 dock-eligible hubs and 0.35 of the 9 actually docked hubs** were recovered per resample, with P(≥3 of 17) = 0.040 and P(≥5 of 17) = 0.000. The single most stable dock-eligible hub was CDHR5 (15.5%). The docking target list is accordingly justified by **structural tractability, not by statistical stability of hub selection**. Two further honesty caveats: λ.1se = 1 gene (sparse linear signal is weak, so hubs rely on ensemble integration, not a single linear model), and the same-animal GSE241361 folds mean those two tests are not independent (some test sets n < 10).

### The human layer is negative—a blood-proxy boundary, not a failure

In GSE158825 (n=60), neither LSS+DS vs LSS (min p = 1.3e-4, FDR 0.128) nor association with %nprs20delta (min p = 8.5e-4, FDR 0.556) reached FDR significance; nominal hits clustered in immune miRNAs (miR-99b/let-7e/miR-125a polycistron; miR-146a-3p; miR-155-5p). The indirect hub-mRNA chain was detectable—miRDB v6.0 predicted **3,511 hub→miRNA targeting relationships spanning 33 of 35 hubs**, of which **752 were high-confidence (score ≥ 80)** and **328 of those pairs involved human-plasma-detectable miRNAs** (253 distinct plasma-detectable miRNAs)—but the **set-level permutation test returned p = 0.51**, i.e. no robust association (the nominal t-significance arose from miRNA non-independence and vanished under permutation). GSE222979 carries no pain phenotype and GSE306403 is not human tissue, so direct human validation is absent. This null is scoped narrowly: it is a **blood-proxy null from plasma miRNA in n = 60 participants**, it is underpowered, and it is uninformative about the DRG–spinal axis itself, which is not sampled by plasma. It demarcates where prospective validation is required rather than confirming an absence of association; prospective clinical qPCR/ELISA is required (see Discussion).

### Hubs are a multi-cellular DRG–spinal programme

On the DRG side (GSE216039), 34/35 hubs were present (CRISP3 absent) and 25 passed detection; **20/25 localised to the injured/regenerating neuron subtype**. Representative enrichment (mean-expression fold, subtype vs other subtypes): SPRR1A detected in 98.1% vs 16.8% of cells (detection-ratio 5.8×) with mean-expression enrichment 17.4×, ECEL1 25.7×, NPY 10.0×, FLNC 10.2×. All "×" values here are mean-expression folds (specificity), distinct from the detection fractions just quoted; they are directional hints given n = 2–3/group (sample-level pseudobulk; see Methods), not inferential estimates. Subtype annotation was robust (leave-one-marker consistency 92–100%, ρ 0.965–0.996), and the eight annotating markers had zero overlap with hubs, ruling out circularity.

On the spinal side (GSE328175), 33/35 hubs were present (CRISP3, REG3B absent), 24 detectable (mean pseudobulk expression > 0 in ≥1 lineage), and **20 were localisable** across neuron/microglia/astrocyte/OPC (15 NotLocalisable). **Only 7/35 achieved cross-dataset lineage-consistent localisation** (5 neuronal: ANKRD13B, CTTN, PTPN23, SRRM4, VASH2; 1 immune: TFE3; 1 glial: CHL1). This is not a localisation failure but evidence of axis-end specialisation: the DRG pole is an injured-neuron programme, the spinal pole disperses across neuronal/glial/immune compartments. Sample-level pseudobulk gave **0 BH-significant genes** in both datasets (n = 2–3/group), so all single-cell calls are directional hints. Ambient-RNA contamination downgraded ATF3 (ambient_index 2.59) and AXL (3.11) spinal assignments; the Cd11b+ GSE246288 non-microglial clusters were voided as ambient artefacts.

Visium spatial mapping (GSE325938) gave the 35 hubs their first spatial coordinates: **33/35 were detectably expressed in mouse spinal cord (CRISP3 and LNP1 were below the detection threshold), and 17 of 33 detectably expressed hubs (51.5%) were assigned to the dorsal horn** on **Sham/baseline tissue—no injury-arm spatial data were available**. Because this is uninjured tissue, the dorsal-horn assignment is reported as **constitutive baseline anatomy**: it shows that these hubs are anatomically present at the pain-afferent first station, not that they are enriched or recruited there by injury. All 17 dorsal-horn assignments passed a 5% detection floor (top-region detection ≥ 5%), below which regional specificity ratios are not interpreted (Methods; Supplementary Table S2). Cross-modal corroboration with snRNA was consistent for 6 hubs, all Neuronal→DorsalHorn (GALNS, SRRM4, PTPN23, VASH2, ANKRD13B, CTTN). Three honesty points accompany this: ATF3 localised to meninges (not dorsal-horn neuron), consistent with its ambient downgrade; the Ependymal region (28% of spots) is likely a central-gray mislabel; and all regionalisation is on Sham tissue, so no injury-induced redistribution is claimed.

### Hub plausibility and annotation outliers

Not every hub carries prior pain/DRG evidence, and several are annotation outliers that we flag rather than conceal. CDHR5 (intestinal epithelial cadherin), ANKRD1 (cardiac ankyrin-repeat protein, CARP), FLNC (muscle filamin C), CRISP3 (secretory/granule protein), and MEGF11 (retinal/spinal-development transmembrane protein) have no established DRG- or pain-role. CDHR5 is the strongest single illustration: it reached full three-method consensus and is consistently up-regulated in all four bulk contrasts (log₂FC +0.07 to +2.62, the largest contribution coming from GSE278227), so it is not a single-study artefact, yet its annotated biology is intestinal-epithelial and no DRG or pain role is established—we therefore treat it as a candidate pending orthogonal confirmation rather than as a finding. Among hubs with established DRG-injury or pain evidence are ATF3, SPRR1A, ECEL1, NPY, VIP and—externally motivated—REG3B. We stress that **REG3B is an external motivational hypothesis, not an internal-supporting hub**: in this study REG3B was *not* in the meta core, was not localisable in spinal snRNA, and showed only 1.7% detection in DRG; its "established DRG neuroimmune hub" status derives solely from a single CRPS-I (not CPSP, not nerve-transection) report{{nie2025}} and must be tested in the present model before any weight is attached.

In the present meta-analysis the purinergic family was itself negative: **P2RX/P2RY showed no coordinate change (permutation p = 0.339 in the bulk-only meta; p = 0.440 in the six-input meta)**. We note this explicitly because the broader literature links microglial P2X4 to neuropathic pain{{tsuda2003}}; the absence of a P2RX/P2RY signal here implies that the DAM-like state captured is only partially the classic P2X4-driven microglial activation described in that literature, and single-cell work after peripheral nerve injury likewise reports only partial, time- and sex-specific overlap with the canonical DAM programme{{tansley2022}}. This is informative about the limits of the axis rather than a contradiction of the literature, and it is one reason we describe the microglial signal as "DAM-like" rather than as a fully reconstituted DAM state.

### Drug repurposing: an honest null at library scale

We docked 3,085 approved drugs against 10 tractable targets (30,850 poses; 30,687 scored) under exhaustiveness-1 (fidelity ceiling ρ ≈ 0.78, supporting candidate prioritisation only). **Reverse positive controls** (ChEMBL binding-type) gave point-estimate AUCs of AXL (0.880), TNIK (0.824), ACVR1 (0.797) and MAPK14 (0.779); these pass as *method-validation* controls (the screen can in principle separate known binders) but, as shown below, none survives the stricter size-independent enrichment test. At the single-variable level, after molecular-weight correction the size-independent increment (ΔAUC versus the MW-only baseline) had a 95% confidence interval that contained zero for both AXL [−0.030, +0.104] and TNIK [−0.224, +0.193], and ACVR1 and MAPK14 matched the MW-only baseline. A stricter multivariate physicochemical control (logistic known-binder ~ MW + logP + TPSA + HBD + HBA + rotB + docking affinity) showed that docking affinity nonetheless still added statistically significant discrimination beyond chemotype for AXL (likelihood-ratio p = 2.6e-5; AUC 0.895 → 0.927), TNIK (p = 7.3e-5; 0.852 → 0.898) and ACVR1 (p = 9.6e-4; 0.898 → 0.931); however, the ACVR1 likelihood-ratio "significance" contradicts its own single-variable size-independent Wald test (**p = 0.584**) and rests on only 9 positive events, so we describe docking signal beyond chemotype as a small, event-poor, n-inflated increment that does not survive the simpler size-independent test rather than as protocol validation; MAPK14 was marginal (p = 0.066) and ADRA2A only weak (p = 0.027; AUC 0.791 → 0.797). The screen therefore cannot claim a validated enrichment for any candidate target, and the honest null applies to the absence of a robust, size-independent candidate hit rather than to a broken method.

**Target-specific biological plausibility is assessed uniformly across all 10 tractable targets** (the author has a pending grant in which ADRA2A is listed among candidate targets; this did not influence the present analyses). For every target, the docking null negates docking-based prioritisation only, never biological plausibility; the table below reports meta_Z, meta_FDR, direction consistency, number of holo PDB structures, and the known biological/pharmacological link for all 10 targets on the same footing. We deliberately make no superlative claim for or against any single target: on the fixed-effect meta-evidence ADRA2A has the smallest |meta_Z| of the ten (4.84), yet under the random-effects meta-analysis only 4 of the 10 targets retained FDR < 0.05—SLC2A1 (q = 0.0000), TNIK (0.021), ADRA2A (0.039) and GALNS (0.025)—so target ranking is itself model-dependent and no target is either privileged or excluded by the meta-analysis.

**The prospectively specified full-library breadth overturned the Tier-1 signal**: ADRA2A reached AUC 0.618 on the 620-drug CNS/analgesic-prior subset (Tier 1) but collapsed to **0.532 (p = 0.118, NS) across all 3,085 drugs**—i.e., non-informative rather than a clean enrichment; after MW adjustment the AUC was 0.578 with a weak but significant ΔAUC (p ≈ 0.0005), so the ADRA2A docking result is inconclusive, not a confirmed null. **Face-validity testing refuted analgesic enrichment**: 0 of 64 known analgesics appeared in the Top-20 (≈0.4 expected), rank AUC ≈ 0.54 (p > 0.14), and α2-agonists scored at chance after MW correction (AUC 0.428, p = 0.76)—consistent with the absence of a selective nonopioid analgesic signal such as suzetrigine{{divito2026}} in this docking space; we note that suzetrigine acts on the expressed channel, so an mRNA-level signal is not expected to translate into docking affinity, and this is a scope limit rather than a discriminatory test. The composite (knowledge-informed) ranking—which blends docking affinity with drug-level pharmacological-prior and accessibility terms—yielded precision@10 = 0.700 (lift ×18.69) and precision@20 = 0.450 when scored against the ADRA2A ChEMBL binder set; these are knowledge-informed *retrieval* metrics, not independent docking evidence, and their hypergeometric p-values (9.4e-9, 1.3e-8) largely restate the prior terms rather than the docking term and are not cited as validating it (pure ADRA2A Vina affinity placed 0/10 known binders in the Top-10, confirming the retrieval is prior-driven, not docking-driven). Among the 10 tractable targets, 8/10 Top-10 identities were stable after MW correction under the full-library composite definition—so we claim a Top-20 *candidate set*, never a single top-ranked drug, and stress that it is not docking evidence for any target. Ion-channel targets, mechanistically important, lacked ligand-anchored holo structures suitable for a defined docking box and were thus outside the structure-based screen.

**Hub→target eligibility rule (explicit).** A hub was eligible for structure-based docking assessment only if it encoded a target with ≥1 ligand-anchored holo PDB structure (n_holo_PDB ≥ 1); this yielded **17 dock-eligible hubs** (ACVR1, AXL, CDHR5, CTTN, FLNC, FLRT3, GALNS, ITPKC, MAPK14, NPY, PTPN23, RUBCN, SERPINE1, SLC2A1, TFE3, TNIK, VASH2). Of these, **9 had a pocket suitable for a defined docking box and were docked** (ACVR1, AXL, GALNS, ITPKC, MAPK14, SERPINE1, SLC2A1, TNIK, VASH2); the tenth docked target was **ADRA2A, which is not a hub and was added as an external analgesic reference**; **TFE3 was dock-eligible but no appropriately ligand-anchored cavity was defined, so it was not docked**. This rule is decoupled from hub-selection stability: because the bootstrap showed 0/35 hubs reached ≥0.9 resampling stability and only 0.79 of the 17 dock-eligible hubs were recovered per resample, the 35-gene set is treated as a candidate set and the docking target list is determined by structural tractability, not by hub rank.

## Discussion

The DRG–spinal axis converges on a neuroimmune–metabolic programme that is best described as a **nerve-injury-associated transcriptional response rather than a CPSP-specific pathway**: coordinated DAM-like microglial and complement activation plus, in the fixed-effect analysis, OXPHOS suppression, consistent with the growing literature on microglial–complement and energy-metabolism dysfunction in chronic and neuropathic pain{{haque2024;yousefpour2025;kong2023;taves2016;coull2005;scholz2007}}, and with prior multi-tissue CPSP resources including a DRG-containing dataset{{meng2024}} and a spinal-cord neuropathic-pain atlas{{dong2025}}. The disease-associated microglia (DAM) signature itself was first defined in neurodegeneration{{kerenshaul2017}} and is increasingly implicated in pain-state microglial reprogramming, though only partially and in a time- and sex-dependent manner{{tansley2022}}; our DAM set follows that provenance and is therefore described as DAM-like. Chemokine signalling at the spinal dorsal horn, notably the CXCL12/SDF-1 axis, has also been implicated specifically in CPSP{{sun2021;luo2016}}, reinforcing the neuroimmune component of the axis. The nerve-injury signature does not predict incision direction (46.3% versus a 47.1% background; difference −0.9 pp, p = 0.14), so specificity of the axis to postsurgical versus nerve-injury pain is not demonstrated by this meta-analysis. This is not a pure ion-channel-null story: although broad ion-channel families did not change coordinately (Nav_SCN q = 0.44), the specific nociceptor sodium channels SCN9A, SCN10A, SCN11A and SCN8A were individually significant and down-regulated in nerve-injury models (SCN8A most significant, meta_Z −4.92, FDR 1.0e-5) but **inverted to up-regulation in the single incision model**—so we explicitly hedge any claim that Nav-channel down-regulation is a CPSP feature, and we bound this inversion as one-study, one-species evidence supported by a single incision arm. Because the channels we find down-regulated are the same ones targeted by conventional Nav-blocking strategies whose clinical efficacy has been limited{{mcdonnell2018}}, the observation is a hypothesis about model-dependent transcriptional direction, not a therapeutic recommendation.

The dual-ML candidate hub set (35 genes; 91% overlapping the meta core) is not a list of "new" genes—ATF3, SPRR1A, NPY, VIP and others are well-established DRG injury markers{{qu2024;pokhilko2020}}. Our contribution is the convergence and prioritisation across independent computational routes under leakage control, and the multi-cellular/spatial localisation showing the programme spans DRG injured neurons and spinal neuron/microglia/astrocyte/OPC compartments, with 17 of 33 detectably expressed hubs anatomically present in the dorsal horn at baseline. We flag REG3B as an external hypothesis only (model-mismatched evidence{{nie2025}}) and the purinergic self-negative (P2RX/P2RY perm p = 0.339) as a boundary on the DAM interpretation.

The honest docking null is itself a methodological contribution. Most virtual-screening repurposing reports stop at Tier-1 enrichment{{gan2023;pham2025}}; we added prospectively specified full-library breadth, reverse positive controls, and MW correction, and showed that (a) ADRA2A's Tier-1 enrichment is non-informative (full-library AUC 0.532, NS; a weak MW-adjusted signal of 0.578, ΔAUC p ≈ 0.0005, is inconclusive), (b) known analgesics are not enriched, and (c) no candidate target cleared both filters we applied—the raw/full-library MW-corrected enrichment p and the size-independent (MW-adjusted) p—and the positive controls (AXL, TNIK, ACVR1, MAPK14) retain docking signal beyond chemotype only in a post-hoc multivariate control (Supplementary Table S4), yet none reaches a robust candidate hit against the full-library baseline. This corrects the "repurposing panacea" narrative and demonstrates how double-dipping produces false positives. The honest null is scoped explicitly to *docking enrichment at library scale*; the separate knowledge-informed Top-20 is a prospectively testable candidate set, not docking evidence, and is framed accordingly.

**We therefore draw no therapeutic priority from this screen.** MAPK14, AXL and TFE3 have the strongest prior biological rationale among the tractable targets, but on our own pre-specified criteria none of them can be advanced: AXL and MAPK14 both failed the size-independent enrichment test (p = 0.141 and 0.579 respectively), and TFE3 was never docked. Naming them as "priorities" would contradict the honest null this paper is built on; they are recorded as hypotheses for which the present screen provides no positive docking evidence, and any ranking among the ten targets is additionally unstable because only 4 of 10 retain random-effects significance. Likewise, ADRA2A is neither prioritised nor excluded: it retains established α2A analgesic pharmacology and remains a plausible hypothesis, but its docking result is inconclusive (full-library AUC 0.532, NS; MW-adjusted 0.578), and its composite-ranking metrics are knowledge-informed retrieval, not docking evidence.

Translational implication: the Top-20 docking candidate set is hypothesis-generating and requires wet-lab validation; no single drug is advocated. Prospective clinical validation of the 35 hubs is the defined next step, but it must be scoped correctly: neuron/glia-restricted hubs (e.g., SPRR1A, SRRM4, ECEL1) are not measurable in peripheral blood and require CSF or tissue; only blood-accessible hubs (e.g., NPY, VIP, SERPINE1, AXL, MAPK14, TFE3) are addressable in the planned plasma qPCR/ELISA work. Direct DRG or spinal-cord biopsy is not clinically obtainable in CPSP patients, so the accessible human biospecimens are peripheral-blood PBMC, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and—where surgically available—post-amputation neuromas or scar-tissue nerve endings; the 35 hubs constitute a rodent axis signature that must be confirmed in human tissue before any therapeutic claim. A limitation shared with all computational CPSP work is the absence of direct human validation; our honestly negative human miRNA layer is a blood-based, underpowered null that demarcates where prospective confirmation is required rather than overstating the animal-to-human inference. We addressed residual physicochemical confounding with a multivariate control (logistic binder ~ MW + logP + TPSA + HBD + HBA + rotatable bonds + docking affinity; Supplementary Table S4): the strongest positive controls (AXL, TNIK, ACVR1) retain docking discrimination beyond chemotype, whereas ADRA2A remains weak and non-informative, leaving the honest-null conclusion unchanged.

## Methods

### Data curation
Twelve GEO datasets were decoded per-dataset for species, model, tissue and true grouping (model × tissue × state). Two original-scheme misclassifications were corrected: GSE222979 is a miRNA dataset (not mRNA) and GSE306403 is the SH-SY5Y cell line (not human tissue). Bulk matrices were unified to gene-symbol × sample; raw counts were transformed to log2(CPM+1) (GSE212311 used its native log2(FPKM+1)). Cross-species symbols were upper-cased; genes absent from ≥3 datasets were excluded from the meta-analysis. Public datasets analysed: GSE267799, GSE212311, GSE278227, GSE241361, GSE265957, GSE158825, GSE222979, GSE216039, GSE328175, GSE246288, GSE306403, GSE325938.

### Meta-analysis
Per dataset, Welch t-tests (unequal variance) with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) FDR were computed. For the DRG-axis meta-analysis, five independent studies are represented by six contrasts because one tibial-nerve-injury study, GSE265957, contributes two ribosome-profiling timepoints (D4 acute and D63 chronic) as separate inputs; GSE265957's DRG input is a *translatome* (Xtail ribosome-profiling log2FC) rather than a bulk transcriptome, mixed with bulk-transcriptome meta_Z from the other studies (a measurement-heterogeneity limitation disclosed here and addressed by the bulk-only sensitivity analysis). The five studies / six contrasts are: GSE267799 chronic vs baseline; GSE212311 CCI; GSE278227 1-week IL/CL; GSE241361 SNI WT DRG; and GSE265957 DRG D4 and D63. Per-contrast Z-values were derived as Z = sign(t)·Φ⁻¹(p/2) from the per-dataset Welch tests (and, for the translatome, from the deposited Xtail p-values).

**Fixed-effect (primary).** A Stouffer weighted Z was computed per gene: Z_meta = Σ(w·Z)/√Σw², with weight w = √(n_case·n_ctrl/(n_case+n_ctrl)) using each contrast's real per-group sample size (GSE267799 12/8 → 2.19; GSE212311 3/3 → 1.22; GSE278227 14/14 → 2.65; GSE241361 DRG 4/5 → 1.49; GSE265957 D4 2/2 and D63 2/2 → 1.00 each). Writing the per-contrast standardised effect as d_i = Z_i/w_i with Var(d_i) = 1/w_i², this weighting is algebraically the inverse-variance (effective-n) weight; it does not re-count sample size. Direction consistency = max(n_up, n_dn)/K (K≥3 required), and because this statistic pools incision with nerve-injury contrasts, a gene can be "core-consistent" while disagreeing with the incision contrast; the two are therefore reported separately (see below). The core signature required meta_FDR < 0.05 and direction consistency ≥ 0.8.

**Random-effects sensitivity (DerSimonian–Laird).** On the same per-contrast effects we computed Q = Σ w_i²(d_i − d_FE)², C = Σ w_i² − Σ w_i⁴/Σ w_i², τ² = max(0, (Q − (K−1))/C) and I² = max(0, (Q − (K−1))/Q)·100, then random-effects weights w*_i = 1/(1/w_i² + τ²) and Z_RE = Σw*_i d_i/√(Σw*_i), with BH FDR across genes. This is reported as the sensitivity bound on the fixed-effect core (Supplementary Table S6).

**Consistency split and non-circular translation test.** For each gene we additionally computed `nerve_injury_consistency` (direction consistency across the five nerve-injury contrasts only, incision excluded; K ≥ 3 required) and `incision_agreement` (sign of the incision log₂FC equals the sign of the meta Z). A *nerve-injury-only* meta-analysis was also run (five contrasts, incision contrast removed from both the Z and the consistency filter), and the incision contrast was then used once as a held-out direction test. Concordance is reported as a proportion with a Wilson 95% confidence interval and tested against an empirical null obtained by randomly re-labelling which genes belong to the test stratum (5,000 draws) rather than against an assumed 50%; the 50% null is inappropriate because the incision contrast has a global directional skew relative to nerve injury.

**Sensitivity analyses.** A collapse-sensitivity meta-analysis merged GSE265957's two timepoints into a single contrast (five inputs); a bulk-only meta-analysis excluded GSE265957 entirely (four bulk studies; core 1,981; overlap 1,732/4,055 = 42.7%). **GSE267799 harvest-day caveat:** the source GEO annotates acute and chronic incision timepoints; the exact chronic harvest day is not stated in the deposited metadata and could not be verified from the reanalysed matrix, so this model's CPSP-adjacency—the reason it is labelled "CPSP-closest"—is asserted by the source, not independently verified here, and the incision→nerve-injury translation is reported as a conservative bound from a single incision arm.

### Gene-set statistics
Nineteen a priori gene sets (neuroinflammation, DAM microglia, complement, mitochondrial OXPHOS, neuropeptides, synaptic, ion-channel families TRP/Nav/CACNA/Kv/KCNQ, MAPK, Sigma-1, opioid GPCR, ASIC, K2P/KCNK, myelin/oligodendrocyte, autophagy/mitophagy, CPSP-literature) were tested on the meta_Z vector by Stouffer combination plus one-sample t, calibrated by **2,000 same-size permutation** tests with a resolution floor of 1/2001 ≈ 0.0005 (values at the floor are reported as an upper bound). **Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied across the 18 multi-member sets** (permutation p and, separately, Stouffer p), because testing 19 sets without set-level correction leaves the three floor-pinned sets tied and ordered only by Stouffer Z; Sigma-1 is a single-member marker, is reported as such, and is excluded from set-level inference and ordering. Sets were additionally recomputed on the random-effects Z-vector. Set members and provenance are listed in Supplementary Table S5; the DAM_microglia set follows Keren-Shaul et al.{{kerenshaul2017}}. The gene-set definition was fixed a priori and not reinterpreted post hoc.

### Dual-ML hub identification (leakage control)
Five DRG-axis datasets (GSE278227, GSE267799, GSE241361 mouse DRG, GSE241361 mouse spinal cord, GSE212311) were intra-dataset gene-z-scored, intersected to 13,208 common genes, and pooled into 72 samples. Univariate t pre-screening (Top 800) fed three feature selectors: LASSO (λ.min, bootstrap B=100), Random Forest mean-decrease-Gini (Top 80), and XGBoost |SHAP| (Top 80). A gene was a **hub** if ≥2/3 methods agreed. Generalisation was assessed primarily by leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) AUC; the **cross-animal mean excludes the two GSE241361 folds**, which are drawn from the same animals and are reported separately (cross-animal floor 0.917, range 0.917–1.000 across three folds). Three of five folds yield degenerate DeLong intervals ([1.0, 1.0]) at small test n, which are reported as non-informative rather than as evidence of perfect precision. A pooled within-dataset 5×20 repeated stratified cross-validation (0.999) is reported only as a leakage-inflated optimistic upper bound, alongside a label-permutation null (0.490 ± 0.085); the repeated-CV and permutation-null statistics are computed in `scripts/p3_ml.py` with a fixed SEED, and per-test-set LODO AUCs are in `results/tables/P3_lodo_auc_ci.csv`. Hub stability was assessed by a 200-resample bootstrap of the 72 pooled samples (`scripts/p3_hub_bootstrap.py`; `results/tables/P3_hub_bootstrap.csv`), and the **stability of the docking target set itself** by a re-run of that bootstrap recording the full recovered hub set per resample (`scripts/p7_targetset_bootstrap.py`; `results/tables/_R4_targetset_bootstrap.csv`), which is the basis for treating the target list as structurally rather than statistically determined.

### Human miRNA layer
GSE158825 (n=60 human plasma miRNA; lumbar surgery + pain outcome %nprs20delta) was tested by Welch+BH and Spearman+BH. miRDB v6.0 predicted hub-mRNA-targeting miRNAs; 3,511 predicted hub→miRNA relationships spanning 33 of 35 hubs were obtained, of which 752 were high-confidence (score ≥ 80) and **328 of those pairs** involved human-plasma-detectable miRNAs (**253 distinct miRNAs**, enumerated in `results/tables/P4_hub_miRNA_human_integration.csv`). Set-level association was tested by 5,000 same-size permutation of miRNAs (p = 0.51). GSE222979 (n=1,242, three body fluids) provided cross-fluid detectability only (no pain phenotype). GSE306403 (SH-SY5Y) was retained as an in-vitro opioid-mechanism reference, not human tissue. This layer is a blood-based proxy and is interpreted only as such.

### Single-cell & spatial localisation
GSE216039 (mouse DRG neuron-enriched scRNA), GSE328175 (mouse lumbar spinal snRNA), and GSE246288 (mouse spinal-cord Cd11b+ microglia scRNA) were analysed with **sample-level pseudobulk only** (cell-level statistics were descriptive, never inferential, to avoid pseudoreplication). Neuron-subtype annotation used eight non-hub markers (GAL, GAP43, SOX11, VGF, MMP16, CDK5R1, SCG2, NCAM1) with zero overlap with the 35 hubs, printed for audit. Ambient-RNA index flagged unreliable cell assignments. Visium GSE325938 (mouse spinal cord) regionalised the 35 hubs on Sham/baseline tissue and cross-modally corroborated snRNA lineage calls. Regional specificity was subject to a detection floor: hubs whose detection fraction in their top region was below 5% (CDHR5, SERPINE1, VIP, REG3B, ANKRD1, plus the all-zero CRISP3 and LNP1) were flagged below-floor and excluded from biological interpretation of regional enrichment, since specificity ratios computed on near-zero detection fractions are noise-driven; per-hub floor status is marked in Supplementary Table S2. Because only Sham tissue was available, dorsal-horn assignment is reported as constitutive baseline anatomy, not injury-induced recruitment.

### Structure-based repurposing (prospectively specified)
The ligand library was ChEMBL max_phase=4 (approved-drug equivalent) → 3,085 pdbqt files (93.2% of 3,311 small molecules). **Parameter-fidelity validation was performed before screening**: exhaustiveness-1 (not max_evals) was chosen because max_evals systematically penalised flexible ligands (Spearman ρ(Δ, rotatable bonds) ≈ +0.26, top-10% recall 0%). Exhaustiveness-1 retained a rank-fidelity ceiling of ρ ≈ 0.78. Ten of 43 candidate targets had dockable holo structures with a ligand-anchored binding site suitable for box definition (GALNS, SLC2A1, TNIK, ITPKC, AXL, SERPINE1, MAPK14, VASH2, ACVR1, ADRA2A); ion-channel candidates (SCN9A/10A/11A/KCNQ2/CACNA2D1/GABRA1) have experimental structures but lack a ligand-anchored pocket appropriate for a defined docking box, and were therefore not docked (they could be revisited by blind/cavity-aware docking). Hub→target eligibility was governed by the explicit rule stated in Results (n_holo_PDB ≥ 1), which selected 17 hubs of which 9 were docked; ADRA2A was added as a non-hub external analgesic reference and TFE3, although eligible, had no definable ligand-anchored cavity. 30,850 dockings (3,085 × 10) were scored in AutoDock Vina 1.2.5. **Reverse positive controls** used ChEMBL binding-type labels (pChEMBL ≥ 6, i.e. ≤1 µM) and are reported as `control_passed` (method-validation) rather than as evidence of enrichment; **molecular-weight confounder correction** applied MW-only baseline, MW-linear-adjusted, and MW-quintile-stratified AUC. Enrichment was declared real only if docking AUC > 0.5, survived correction, and exceeded the MW-only baseline. A multivariate physicochemical control extended the single-variable MW correction: for each of the five ChEMBL-annotated targets a logistic model of known-binder status on six descriptors (MW, logP, TPSA, HBD, HBA, rotB) was fit, and a likelihood-ratio test assessed whether docking affinity added residual discrimination (Supplementary Table S4). Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the five targets was applied to both the raw full-library enrichment p-values and the size-independent (MW-adjusted) p-values (Supplementary Table S4): 4/5 raw p-values survive BH, whereas only ADRA2A's weak size-independent p survives BH, and its full-library AUC remains 0.532 (p = 0.118, NS)—so a covariate-adjusted increment above the MW baseline is not interpreted as enrichment when the full-library AUC is itself indistinguishable from chance. For AXL, where a breadth-table PASS (0.880) coexists with a size-independent p of 0.141 whose confidence interval contains zero, **the size-independent test is declared authoritative** and the breadth-table value is reported as a method-validation control only; the two numbers answer different questions and are not both treated as enrichment verdicts.

### Multiple-testing registry
Correction was applied within, not across, analytical families, and the families are reported separately so that no single claim rests on an uncorrected test: (i) per-dataset differential expression — BH across genes; (ii) meta-analysis — BH across genes, fixed and random effects separately; (iii) gene sets — BH across the 18 multi-member sets (added in this version); (iv) docking — BH across the five ChEMBL-annotated targets for both raw and size-independent p; (v) single-cell — sample-level BH, with all cell-level statistics descriptive. Composite-ranking hypergeometric p-values are reported as descriptive retrieval metrics and are explicitly not counted among the corrected inferential tests.

### Statistical discipline, causal scope and AI-use disclosure
Single-cell differential expression was never inferred at the cell level (pseudoreplication); only sample-level pseudobulk Welch+BH across n = 2–3/group was reported, and all such localisation findings are labelled "directional hints". AUC-based enrichment p-values are one-sided; permutation-calibration p-values are two-sided, as stated in the Reporting Summary. **Causal scope:** this is an observational reanalysis of public transcriptomes with no intervention, no temporal ordering within tissue, and no perturbation; all findings are associations, and terms such as "response" or "programme" are used descriptively rather than mechanistically. Accordingly, no causal claim—including any claim that the observed neuroimmune-metabolic coordination causes, maintains, or can be targeted to modify chronic postsurgical pain—is made or supported by this design. A large language model (LLM) was used to assist manuscript drafting and language polishing; the scientific design, analyses, results and conclusions were conceived and verified by the author, and no LLM satisfies authorship criteria.
